# Every listing is a training environment

*How we put a humanoid robot to work inside a home generated from real-estate photos — in one day, and what broke along the way.*

**Spatial Intelligence + Generative 3D Hackathon · San Francisco · 5 September 2026**
**Team:** npow · abhijitbetigeri · cosmicz · **Track:** Physical AI & Simulation

---

## The problem we started from

Home robots learn in homes. The homes they learn in today come from a few thousand scanned houses — Matterport3D, HM3D, Zillow's ZInD — captured with rigs, gated behind research agreements, and approved by hand. We tried to get ZInD the morning of the hackathon. The form said *up to two weeks*.

Meanwhile there are millions of listings on the market, each with eight photos.

So the question we set ourselves was narrow: **can a listing's photos become a room a robot can actually work in?** Not a pretty render — a metric, physics-ready scene with a task you can score.

By 6 pm we had a real 29-DOF Unitree G1 walking a room generated by World Labs Marble, taking a book off one of the room's own shelves, carrying it across, and placing it on the empty shelf on the other wall. The video is [here](https://abhijitbetigeri.github.io/robot-pick-place/). This post is about how, and about the nine things that went wrong on the way — because they're the useful part.

## The pipeline

Four tools, each doing one job that the others can't:

- **World Labs Marble** takes photos and returns a persistent 3D world. It exports three representations: a Gaussian splat (`.spz`, the photoreal one), a collider mesh (`.glb`, for physics), and a high-quality textured mesh (`.glb` with UVs and a baked 8192² texture).
- **Mint** generates objects from text through a REST API — a bookshelf, an armchair, a plant — which we rescale to metric size and give collision proxies.
- **Convex** holds the room layout as live shared state. Every furniture placement is a document, so two people can stage one room and watch each other's drags.
- **MuJoCo** runs the robot. The staged layout becomes a scene, a planner routes the base around whatever was placed, and a scripted task moves the book.

Stage a room in the browser, press **Run**, get a rollout back in about ten seconds.

## What broke, in the order it broke

### 1. `.gitignore` was eating the source

Our first afternoon discovery wasn't about robots. The repo's `.gitignore` had `data/`, which matches at *any* depth — so `src/realestate/data/` had been silently ignored since the first commit. `git status` showed nothing. The app built perfectly on one laptop and on nobody else's. We changed it to `/data/`. If you take one thing from this post, take that.

### 2. A splat is not a surface

The Gaussian splat is the beautiful representation, and it is useless for physics. It's 1.9 million semi-transparent ellipsoids with colour and no surfaces — nothing to collide with. No engine can simulate against it. Physics always runs on the collider mesh; the splat is for looking.

### 3. The collider is an open shell, and MuJoCo collides by convex hull

Marble's collider is a shell, not a solid. MuJoCo collides meshes by their convex hull, which turns the *inside* of a room into a solid block. Load it naively and the robot cannot enter. We use the mesh as visual geometry and derive box colliders from its bounds — and, later, detect shelf surfaces from the mesh directly.

### 4. Y-up, Z-up, and an origin at the ceiling

glTF is Y-up; MuJoCo is Z-up. Rotate 90° about X or your room simulates lying on its side, and nothing complains. Then we aligned the splat to the collider by reusing the collider's translation — and the room sat 2.8 m below the robot. `trimesh` bakes the GLB's node transform on load; the raw `.spz` keeps the exporter's frame, whose origin was at ceiling height. Same world, two origins. Measure each file's frame separately.

### 5. Never fit to a splat's bounding box

Splats carry stray outliers. Our 4.4 × 5.2 × 2.8 m bedroom had a raw bounding box of 11.5 × 20.4 × 17.2 m. Fitting to it scaled the whole world wrongly. Percentile extents (0.5–99.5) gave 4.44 × 5.16 × 2.83 — within 10% of the collider.

### 6. The grasp that worked from across the room

Our first robot was a Stretch-style mobile manipulator, and its "grasp" was a weld constraint. On the first run the trace said *success*: the book was on the far shelf. The video showed the book floating 1.8 m from the gripper the whole way. The weld had happily grabbed it from across the room. We added a proximity check that raises rather than welds — and it caught two more wrong approach poses before lunch.

### 7. MuJoCo re-centres mesh vertices at compile time

When we switched to the real G1 and tried to replay it in the browser, every link drew in the wrong place. We'd shipped the original STL files and driven them with `geom_xpos` from MuJoCo — but MuJoCo re-centres mesh vertices when it compiles, and poses are expressed against *those* vertices. Exporting geometry straight out of the compiled model (`mesh_vert`, `mesh_face`) made poses and vertices consistent by construction.

### 8. The room had bookshelves on every wall

To texture the room we first projected the 360° panorama onto the collider — it got us from grey clay to colour, but a panorama is one viewpoint, and everything it couldn't see smeared. Marble's high-quality mesh (597k faces, UVs, an 8192² baked texture) fixed it properly, rendered natively by MuJoCo.

Then the camera vanished. In a 2.5 m-wide room lined with real bookshelves, 45 of 48 candidate camera positions sat *inside* furniture. We stopped choosing cameras by hand: the script scans the centre corridor for positions clear of geometry with full coverage and picks the one farthest from the robot's path. Same idea for the shelves — upward-facing faces near the walls, clustered by height; the largest continuous board (0.29 m², nothing breaking its surface) is the empty one.

### 9. Photoreal is a rendering problem, not a simulation problem

We spent an hour trying to render the splat ourselves. `gsplat` is CUDA-only and disables itself on Apple Silicon; Isaac Sim doesn't run on a Mac at all; a custom Spark viewer never converged before the deadline. The lesson was to stop conflating two things: the *physics* needs the collider, the *look* needs either the textured mesh (works in MuJoCo) or the splat (works only in a browser). Once we separated them, the textured mesh was the answer.

## What's real and what's scripted

We want to be exact about this, because a robotics audience will ask.

**Real:** the room (generated, textured, its own shelves), the objects (generated), the layout (shared, live), the 29-DOF G1 model and meshes, the path planning, the occlusion, the metric scale, the success check (the book must end resting on the target surface).

**Scripted:** the gait. Joint angles come from a parametric walk cycle and the root follows the planned path; the scene advances with `mj_forward`. The trained policies we had were all mountaineering — fixed-line slopes, self-arrest, slip recovery — and none transfer to indoor walking with a manipulation objective. Training one is hours of GPU, not an afternoon. *"Scripted gait on the real G1 model"* is an accurate claim. *"The robot learned to walk here"* is not.

## Why this matters

World Labs published a real-to-sim-to-real result this year: one real demonstration, thousands of simulated variations, policies that transferred to five robot platforms without real-world training data — and, critically, simulated evaluation that predicted real-world rankings. Their inputs were captured scenes. Ours are listing photos. If the environment side of that loop can come from photos anyone already has, the bottleneck moves from *access to homes* to *quality of generation* — and generation is getting better every quarter.

## What we'd do next

1. **Learn the gait.** Flat-ground locomotion for the G1 in these rooms, then the manipulation. The environments are cheap now; the policy is the work.
2. **Register the splat to the physics frame** so the photoreal view and the simulation share coordinates without a human nudging them.
3. **Physics against the real shelf geometry**, via convex decomposition of the textured mesh, instead of box proxies.
4. **Isaac Sim.** The scene exports to USD today; running it needs NVIDIA hardware we didn't have at the table.
